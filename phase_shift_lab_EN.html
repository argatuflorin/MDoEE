01 · Core idea

What is phase shift?

When two sinusoidal signals have the same frequency, phase shift is the angular displacement between them. It tells us whether one signal reaches the same event earlier or later than the other.

u1(t)=A1sin(2πft)
u2(t)=A2sin(2πft+φ)

In this lesson, φ > 0 means signal u2 leads signal u1. If φ < 0, then u2 lags.

Why it matters in monitoring and diagnosis: phase relationships help engineers understand power factor, filter behavior, sensor conditioning, resonance, and abnormal equipment behavior in electrical systems.

One full cycle corresponds to 360° = 2π rad. A quarter cycle corresponds to 90°. If frequency changes while phase angle remains fixed, the equivalent time shift changes.

Important distinction: phase shift is not the same as amplitude difference, and it is not frequency mismatch. If frequencies differ, the phase angle does not remain constant — it slips over time.

03 · From angle to measurement

How is phase shift measured with an oscilloscope?

1
Connect both signals to separate channels and use a proper reference/ground arrangement.
2
Choose two equivalent points on the two waveforms, such as zero-crossings with the same slope.
3
Measure the period T and the time difference Δt.
4
Convert time into angle using φ = -360°·Δt/T in the sign convention used in this simulator.
Equivalent expression: φ = 2πfΔt with sign chosen according to the lead/lag definition.
Common mistake: comparing non-equivalent points, such as a zero-crossing on one waveform with a peak on the other waveform. This gives a wrong angle.

05 · Student activity

Guided lab tasks and screenshot evidence

A practical way to use this HTML in class is to ask students to set the required parameters, then take screenshots that clearly show the controls and the result. Each screenshot becomes evidence that the task was completed correctly.
Task 1 · Signal explorer

Lead/lag identification

Set f = 50 Hz, A₁ = A₂ = 1, and adjust the phase shift so that u₂ lags u₁ by 90°.

Deliver: one screenshot and the value of Δt in ms.

Task 2 · RC

Observe an RC low-pass response

Choose R = 1 kΩ and C = 100 nF. Set a frequency above cutoff and observe how the output amplitude and phase change.

Deliver: one screenshot and one sentence: does the output lead or lag?

Task 3 · RL

Inductor output behavior

Set R = 100 Ω, L = 100 mH, f = 100 Hz. Determine whether the output across the inductor leads or lags the input.

Deliver: one screenshot and the output phase angle.

Task 4 · RLC

Find resonance

Using the default series RLC values, move the operating frequency until the current becomes maximal and the phase approaches 0°.

Deliver: one screenshot and the measured resonant frequency.

Task 5 · Design challenge

Target a specific cutoff

Choose values of R and C such that the RC cutoff frequency is close to 1 kHz.

Deliver: the selected R and C, plus a screenshot.

Task 6 · Diagnostic thinking

Interpretation

Explain, in one or two sentences, why resonance can be dangerous in practical electrical equipment.

Deliver: a short written explanation.

06 · Case studies

Why these ideas matter in equipment monitoring and diagnosis

Capacitor banks and power factor

Phase shift between voltage and current is central to reactive power compensation. Monitoring phase angle helps detect abnormal compensation or capacitor degradation.

Sensor conditioning circuits

RC and RL networks are frequently used in analog conditioning. Their phase and magnitude behavior affect timing, filtering, and diagnosis of sensor signal quality.

Resonance in installations

Unexpected resonance may amplify current or voltage, create overheating, disturb measurements, and accelerate the aging of components.
