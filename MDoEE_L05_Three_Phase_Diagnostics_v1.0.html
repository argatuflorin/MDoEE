01 · Learning objectives

What will you investigate?

A four-wire three-phase system has sinusoidal phase voltages with 120° separation. When the three phase currents are sinusoidal and balanced, their instantaneous sum is zero. When fundamental currents become unbalanced or zero-sequence harmonic components are present, a neutral current appears.

Learning outcomes

  1. Explain the three-phase reference system and the neutral current.
  2. Distinguish fundamental unbalance from harmonic distortion.
  3. Compute RMS, harmonic distortion THD-F and neutral RMS.
  4. Interpret current patterns as diagnostic indicators — not confirmed faults.
  5. Compare a calculated prediction with values from the HTML model.

Laboratory sequence · 120 min

20 min: definitions, equations and predictions.

70 min: four experiments, data recording and calculations.

30 min: diagnostic explanations, evidence screenshots and submission.

Deliver: worksheet, one worked calculation, 4-case table, three meaningful screenshots, conclusions.

Safety and model scope: this is a theoretical/simulated signal model, not an instrument connected to an installation. Nothing here authorizes student wiring or measurements on energized equipment.
02 · Diagnostic lens

How to read the evidence

Balanced regime

Normal reference under the defined conditions. No neutral-current indicator.

Fundamental unbalance

Nonzero neutral RMS may prompt phase-by-phase current and loading checks.

H3 / H5 distortion

Elevated THD and triplen harmonics call for load-type, neutral loading and power-quality investigation.

Interpretations depend on the model. A nonzero neutral current or elevated THD is not, by itself, proof that a particular component has failed.

05 · Theory, interpretation and limitations

Calculation reference

Reference equations and convention

Assumptions: ideal symmetrical three-phase sinusoidal voltage source, f₁ = 50 Hz, connected ideal neutral. Current harmonics are imposed by an educational load-spectrum model, not obtained from a rectifier circuit. Phase offsets: θA = 0°, θB = −120°, θC = +120°.

iₖ(t) = √2·I₁,ₖ [sin(ωt+θₖ) + a₃sin(3ωt+3θₖ) + a₅sin(5ωt+5θₖ)]

Here I₁,A = I₁,C = I; I₁,B = kI; a₃ = H3/100; a₅ = H5/100. Each harmonic percentage refers to the fundamental of its own phase. Neutral current iN(t) = −[iA(t)+iB(t)+iC(t)].

RMS, distortion and neutral current
Iphase,RMS = I₁·√(1+a₃²+a₅²) ; THD-F = 100·√(a₃²+a₅²) %

The neutral current is found by vector summation separately for each harmonic order and then root-sum-square across different orders. For balanced identical phases: fundamental and fifth-harmonic neutral components cancel; H3 triplen components add in the neutral. Thus IN,RMS = 3I₁a₃ for a balanced H3-only case.

Diagnostic reading without overclaiming

A nonzero neutral current under fundamental unbalance is a clue to investigate unequal phase loading. A high third harmonic in a four-wire installation can raise the neutral RMS even when fundamental currents are balanced. High THD can justify checking nonlinear loads and heating. Confirmation requires validated field measurements, loading context, instrument accuracy, trend data and applicable acceptance criteria.

Limits of this model

Only H1, H3 and H5 are modeled; no DC, interharmonics, neutral impedance, voltage distortion, phase impedance, transient behavior or instrument measurement uncertainty. Voltage is not calculated from a circuit load; harmonic phase angles follow a prescribed waveform-shift convention. RMS and THD are computed directly from known components (not an FFT estimate). Agreement with the formula checks implementation consistency, not experimental validation.
